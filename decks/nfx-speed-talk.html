The Speed Talk (NFX)

The Speed Talk

James Currier, NFX · How to think about speed in a startup

Why this talk exists

Ten years of writing and rehearsing. NFX gives it to every founder it backs. It is their most-watched video, and many founders have each new employee watch it on day one.

The thesis

Speed is the number one competitive advantage in startup life. Most of what slows you down is in your mind, and it can be fixed.

Part 1

Why speed wins

What NFX looks for in founders

Not a long checklist. Just speed.

  • They reply to email fast
  • They change the marketing plan fast
  • They change the spreadsheets fast

You can feel speed when it’s there: people aren’t encumbered. They’re just going.

You’re an X-wing. They’re Death Stars.

In 1994 there were a few incumbents, like Microsoft and AOL. Now you face lots of Death Stars, and you’re in a star fighter. Your only advantage is speed.

“We’re going in full throttle”

That’s Luke Skywalker’s call as he attacks the Death Star. Full-throttle speed is the only advantage he has.

Your team needs to be Wedge

When Luke goes full throttle, Wedge doesn’t ask “Are you sure? Can we talk about this?” He says: “I’m right with you, boss.”

Wedge was the only other pilot to survive.

Two moves for every one of theirs

If you move twice as fast, or 3×, 5×, 20× faster than your competitors, you can beat anybody who is bigger or smarter. That’s your advantage.

What speed buys you

  • A better product, faster: rapid activity beats the competition
  • More fun: the team accomplishes more
  • Attention: press, investors and future employees follow a company with lots of announcements
  • Valuation goes up

Part 2

Why people don’t go faster

Three barriers, mostly in your mind

  1. Your speed bar is set too low
  2. You’re too fearful
  3. You’re not making things simple enough

From four venture-backed startups, roughly 300 investments, a 75-company accelerator, a venture firm and an incubator.

Barrier 1: It’s not your fault

Your speed bar was set too low by your own success.

  • School is incredibly slow, and you were good at it
  • Corporations don’t reward going fast; it scares people, who need to be informed and tweak things

You thought you were learning school or Google. You were learning to go too slow.

Success makes you cautious

Less successful people go faster because they have nothing to lose. You feel you have a lot to lose, and you think you know how the world works.

You didn’t get it wrong. The people around you did. Reset your speed bar.

Mike Cassidy, the founder of speed

In the 1990s, a startup typically took about 24 months:

  • 6 months to explore ideas
  • 3 months to raise money
  • 3 months to hire the core team
  • 13 months to build
  • 3 months to launch

Cassidy did it in 4 months.

Cassidy: raise money in two days

All the investor meetings on the same day. At the end of each: “I’ve got six other meetings today. Here are the terms. Let me know tonight.”

Two or three term sheets by evening. A winner the next day.

Cassidy: hire in two weeks

At the end of the interview, the offer is ready with the price. “You have 24 hours to sign. You start tomorrow.”

He coached James through his first startup in the late 1990s: “Why does it take you that long?”

The incubator: one-tenth the cost and time

In 2007, after their first exit, they started an incubator: 45 people in one room, building four products at once.

They built 20 products for one-tenth of the money and one-tenth of the time competitors were spending.

A game in 21 days

Hired gaming experts said a good game would take 12–14 months. There were maybe 35 days.

A web engineer who had never made a game said: “I’ll build it in 21 days. No meetings. Give me half a designer.”

He didn’t know he couldn’t

He delivered a working game in 21 days. Three weeks later it had 3 million users and earned real revenue.

He worked nights and weekends, and had a great time doing it.

Dragons of Atlantis

Same company, $600K left, about to go out of business. The estimate for a serious game: $4M and 12 months.

The team built it in 5 months for $450K. The next year it made $120M in revenue.

Release four times a day

Dragons of Atlantis had 45 million registered users and up to 3 million concurrent users. The team still released four times a day.

Most firms release every week or two. If your team says it can’t release daily, it can do better.

Daily releases change minds

In the accelerator, it took weeks to convince founders they could release every day. Once they did, their thinking opened up and everything accelerated.

Sailing the Atlantic at 16

Most people take 5 years and $350K to prepare for a solo Atlantic crossing.

James’s son learned to sail in March, left in June in a $12K boat, and finished at the end of July: 7 months, about $20K.

Others will call it dangerous

The people who spent $350K say it’s dangerous or wrong. It’s because their speed bar is set too low.

Don’t worry about your age, your experience or what everyone says it will take.

How often do you experiment?

Hardware is harder. In software, run 20 to 100 experiments a month.

Dragons of Atlantis ran 20 to 100 a week. If you can’t do 20 a month, you’re not in the game.

Built in days and months

  • Direct Hit: built in 3 months, sold for $500M after 500 days
  • Xfire: built in 3 months, sold to MTV about 12 months later for $110M
  • Hot or Not: built in 8 days by a company named Eight Days
  • Meerkat and Clubhouse: 30-day projects

Pick what fits the time

James Hong didn’t ask what to build. He asked what he could get done in 8 days. If it couldn’t be done in 8 days, they didn’t build it.

Running out of money is a tactic

With 30 days of cash left and a pitch in 30 days, James’s team designed, built and launched a product in 30 days. He raised $7.5M.

The deadline was an external force, not the boss cracking the whip.

Your speed bar can be much higher

If people tell you it can’t be done, you now have examples. You’ll see more once you start doing it yourself.

Part 3

Fear is the main barrier: 12 ways around it

1. The product is not you

In your head, the product is your soul: if people love it, they’ll love you.

It’s not you. It’s a bunch of pixels, a feature, a start. Get it out the door.

2. Don’t try to get it right

Product people want it perfect. Go incremental: get it up, measure it, iterate.

Roughly 10% product, 50% metrics, 40% onboarding flow. Getting it right is what big companies are for.

3. Build the skateboard first

Don’t build a wheel, then a chassis, then a body, then the car. Nothing moves until the end.

Build a skateboard, then a push scooter, a bicycle, a motorcycle, and finally the car.

Why the skateboard wins

You look silly on a skateboard while others drive. But you’re actually moving, so you’ll iterate to the car faster than anybody else.

People he has coached come back to this diagram year after year.

4. Launch behind the scenes

If your company is NFX, don’t launch on nfx.com. Launch on another domain to a small group.

One fearful team relaxed once its broken experiments lived on other URLs that friends wouldn’t see.

5. Run experiments, don’t play business

Founders idolize the end state and play business, like spending months on how to run a board meeting.

You’re not building a business yet. You’re running a series of experiments that will look like a skateboard.

6. Think like a studio: Meerkat

The team built for 14 months, then for 8 months on a seven-feature plan. Neither worked.

In 90 minutes at a whiteboard they picked five ideas, each with a color, a name and one or two features, to launch one every 30 days.

Why the studio works

If you know the next one ships 30 days after this one, you stop over-caring about this one.

Meerkat took 60 days instead of 8 or 14 months, and took off before Twitter shut it down. The next one, built in 30 days, became Houseparty.

7. Your job is to learn

Your job is to run experiments and learn. The question each day is: “What did you learn today?”

8. Borrow what already works

Copy what works. Don’t debate it. Need a calendar? Copy the best calendar.

You’ll still create something unique, because you have a unique mind and situation.

Same code, different outcomes

Two teams launched the exact same product from the same code. In six months one reached 53 million people, the other 47 million.

One stayed a photo-sharing product. The other became Bebo, a social network that sold for $850M.

The giants borrowed too

  • Google: copied nearly everything; AdSense was bought
  • Facebook: Friendster, down to the blue
  • Airbnb: took off when it became more like VRBO, at a 10% fee
  • Uber: copied Lyft within 30 days
  • Lyft: Sidecar invented it but was too slow

9. The “f*** it” mentality

His best business moments came with his back against the wall, out of money and not sleeping. Then he gave up the fear and built what the market needed.

Don’t wait for the last 30 to 60 days of cash. Start that way.

10. Watch for self-sabotage

Many founders start companies to prove something. Elon Musk’s friend said the one sign was a father who told him he’d never amount to anything.

If part of you feels unworthy, you may be quietly sabotaging yourself while looking busy.

Slowness is often self-sabotage

Mapping founders, speed and success are clearly correlated. The slow ones’ psychology is mostly self-sabotage.

Learn about it, and call it out in your co-founders.

11. Stop being a wimp: decide

When it isn’t working, don’t hedge or keep the old product while starting a new one. Half measures are self-sabotage.

Move the whole company now. Founders who do beat those who hedge.

12. Do it for them

If you won’t go fast for yourself, do it for the employees, teammates and investors counting on you. You owe it to them.

Part 4

Simplify and go where the action is

Don’t innovate in the basics

  • Incorporate in Delaware
  • Use standard, simple contracts
  • Innovate in your customer and go-to-market, not in paperwork

Keep the terms vanilla

Avoid warrants, milestones, contingencies, first rights and participation. One founder team that ignored this got a board member trying to take the company over.

Take $18M vanilla over $28M with strings.

No partnerships, no bizdev

His first investor made him promise: no partnerships. After four weeks of trying, he saw it: endless talks, negotiation, implementation and conflicting interests.

You cannot bizdev your way to a big business.

Simplify the product

  • Focus on the metrics
  • Stay open to a route at one-sixth the cost and time
  • Ask how basic it can be: Twitter, LinkedIn, Facebook and Google all launched with simple first versions

Do less software

Find ways to get it done without building software.

Instead of a week or two of building, they renamed a button and dropped a whole section, then kept experimenting.

Choose what not to do

  • No QA
  • No usability studies or focus groups: ship it and watch the metrics
  • No politics: nothing left unsaid
  • A three-person board that the founders control
  • No PR early: obscurity lets you iterate
  • Zero marketing spend
  • No budget process before 30 people

Go where the action is

The biggest waste is building something people don’t want. Someone has tried your idea.

Do the research. Take a competitor’s former VP of sales to lunch. Founders who meet six such people say it transforms their thinking.

Find the fast-moving water

Growing 10% a month and not fired yet? That’s not good enough. Aim for 70% a month.

Pull in resources fast: Zynga

On the Facebook platform, Zynga bought Texas Hold’em Poker, raised $35M and hired about 150 people in two months.

With money, press and bought traffic, it built a network effect business once worth $11B.

Recruit in three weeks

Make the offer before candidates leave the interview. Give yourself at most three weeks to fill a slot. Use the new AI recruiting tools.

Part 5

Your team and your tactics

Kick ass, and get your ass kicked

Welcome being challenged by the market, investors and co-founders, or you won’t change fast enough.

This is a gladiator sport, not a career path. Be competitive and decisive.

“Sauvage”

Emily Leproust, founder of Twist Bioscience, did the patents, the fundraising, sales and hiring.

She looks for founders who are sauvage: not evil, but decisive, aggressive and fast.

Keep the team small

Every new person adds conversations and needs: to belong, be invited and be heard. Those needs slow you down.

The right four engineers beat 35. Stay humble and don’t be too attached to the answers.

Cheap, fast and good go together

His last VP of product said: “Cheap, fast or good: pick two.” He fired him and never hired another.

Something cheap and fast can usually be iterated until it’s good.

Stoke: the math of speed

The rocket company burns about $80K a day. Saving one day saves $80K, so paying 20% more to get a part 5 days sooner is a yes.

Going faster also means more experiments, which gets to higher quality.

Speed is not working more hours

Speed, quality and cost reinforce each other.

Going fast is about removing your mental and operational barriers, not burning yourself out. You were taught the wrong things.

Commandos, soldiers and police

  • Commandos take the hill: cost 150%, deliver 10×
  • Soldiers calm things down: cost 110%, deliver 3×
  • Police make sure nothing goes wrong: cost 100%, deliver less than 1×

Hire commandos for your first thousand people.

Soldiers bring in police

Steve Jobs: B players bring in C players. A soldier brings in police, who make the soldier look bold.

Eight good people and three slow ones? Let the slow ones go.

Tactics: time and network

  • Set the time, not the output: what can we ship before bed?
  • Keep building your network
  • One call, one answer, move on: don’t turn a question into a dinner

Tactics: psychology and priorities

Boards paid James to help CEOs with growth. The work was always psychology and prioritization.

Unicorn founders need help with both. So do you.

Tactics: a priority dashboard

Project it on the wall and change it together every Monday. Anyone with a pet project must justify moving it up.

About 40 minutes a week; works well up to 30 people.

Tactics: feedback by lunchtime

Skip the focus group. Take a laptop onto the street, show 8 to 10 people, watch what they click, and fix it that afternoon.

Or use an email group: Gmail had about 80 testers for 14 months.

Tactics: the minimum experiment

Get the answer without building the product.

  • Paper mock-ups: Outdoorsy asked 10; 9 wanted it, and it grew into a $2B company
  • Landing pages: do they click? Do they enter a card?
  • Announce first: Bill Gates’s vaporware kept rivals unfunded

More minimum experiments

  • Explainer video: Dropbox’s 3-minute narrated mock-up took the waiting list from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight
  • Email list or form: Zappos bought shoes from a store and shipped them for its first 2,000 orders

Tactics: external deadlines

People believe a deadline from a customer or demo day more than one from you. That’s why Y Combinator works.

Accelerator founders did 12 to 18 months of work in three, with an excuse to tell everyone else to back off.

Tactics: outsource and share the numbers

  • Outsource everything you can, and use AI
  • As few processes as possible, except trust, safety and payments
  • Email everyone yesterday’s metrics daily, so people act without you in the room

What slows founders down

  • Remote work: under 100 people, be in the same room
  • Acquisition talks that flatter your ego
  • An incomplete team for more than three weeks, or the wrong team
  • Wanting to be proven right about your first idea

More things that slow you down

  • No focus on one key customer
  • Maintaining two products
  • Not renaming the company when you need to
  • A crowded cap table (he had 16 angels)
  • Too little capital: aim for 12 to 24 months of runway
  • Complex terms and a bad investor on the board

Make a religion of speed

Reset your speed bar. Get around your fear. Simplify everything. Then go in full throttle.
